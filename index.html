Abstract

When multimodal large language models (MLLMs) are deployed in design workflows, they routinely encounter scenarios where textual instructions and visual specifications carry contradictory information. This paper introduces DesignSpec-Bench, a pilot benchmark designed to systematically evaluate how MLLMs handle such conflicts in design specification contexts. We construct a taxonomy of five conflict types spanning normative, dimensional, material, color, and layout contradictions, and annotate model responses across four state-of-the-art MLLMs—Claude, Gemini, Qwen, and Doubao—using a three-category behavior coding scheme (Clarification, Text-priority, Silent execution). Our analysis reveals a dominant silent execution pattern (overall SER ≈ 60%), significant inter-model variation in modality bias (MBI range: 33%–88%), and a quality gap between models that clarify conflicts versus those that silently execute. Inter-annotator reliability is high (Fleiss κ = 0.90 for behavior, κ = 0.66 for modality preference). These findings suggest that conflict-handling behavior is a meaningful and underexplored dimension of MLLM evaluation in applied design contexts.

Research Questions
RQ1

Behavior Patterns

What conflict-handling behavior patterns do MLLMs exhibit when facing text–image contradictions in design specification tasks?

RQ2

Modality Preference

When MLLMs resolve conflicts without clarification, do they systematically prefer one modality over another, and does this vary by conflict type?

RQ3

Model Differences

How do different MLLMs differ in their conflict-handling strategies, and what are the quality implications of these differences?

Benchmark Design
Conflict Taxonomy
SC

Style Conflict

The textual specification and visual reference describe incompatible aesthetic directions (e.g., "minimalist, high-end" brief with a dense, retro-style reference image).

EC

Element Conflict

Required elements exceed spatial or compositional constraints, or element positioning requirements are mutually contradictory.

RC

Requirement Conflict

Logical, physical, or temporal constraints are mutually exclusive (e.g., nine deliverables required by the next morning).

NC

Normative Conflict

Design requirements conflict with brand guidelines, legal regulations, or cultural conventions.

PC

Priority Conflict

Multiple objectives are assigned equal or contradictory priority, making visual hierarchy determination impossible.

Results
01

Overall Behavior Distribution

Across all four models and five conflict types, silent execution (S-behavior) is the dominant response strategy, accounting for approximately 60% of all coded responses. This pattern holds consistently across conflict categories, suggesting that MLLMs are broadly inclined to proceed without surfacing ambiguity rather than seeking clarification from the user.

Clarification behavior (C) is rare overall but shows meaningful inter-model variation. Claude and Gemini exhibit substantially higher clarification rates compared to Qwen and Doubao, whose silent execution rates reach 76% and 84% respectively. The distribution of T-behavior (text-priority silent execution) is more evenly spread, indicating that when models do resolve conflicts implicitly, text tends to win.

02

Modality Bias Polarization

Among responses that resolve conflicts without clarification, models show strongly polarized modality preferences. The Modality Bias Index (MBI) — the proportion of non-C responses that follow the text modality — ranges from 33% (Qwen) to 88% (Claude), a spread of 55 percentage points. This polarization indicates that modality preference is a stable, model-level characteristic rather than a context-driven decision.

Claude and Gemini are strongly text-biased (MBI 88% and 78%), while Qwen shows a near-balanced or slight image preference (MBI 33%). Doubao occupies an intermediate position (MBI 67%). Notably, the inter-annotator agreement for modality coding is lower than for behavior coding (Fleiss κ = 0.66 vs. 0.90), reflecting genuine ambiguity in cases where models blend both modalities.

03

WCHS Quality Gap

The Weighted Conflict-Handling Score (WCHS) reveals a substantial quality gap between models that clarify conflicts and those that silently execute. Claude achieves the highest WCHS (42.2%), followed by Gemini (30.6%), Qwen (17.5%), and Doubao (8.3%). This ordering mirrors the clarification rate ranking, suggesting that the willingness to surface conflicts is a strong predictor of overall response quality in design specification tasks.

A Spearman correlation between SER and WCHS across models yields ρ = −0.19 (pooled, n.s., p = 0.064, n = all items), indicating a negative but non-significant trend at the item level. The relationship is clearer at the model level: the two models with the lowest SER (Claude, Gemini) consistently outperform the two with the highest SER (Qwen, Doubao) on WCHS. The scatter plot shows that high-SER responses cluster at low WCHS values, while C-coded responses span a wider quality range.

Discussion
1. Normative-Conflict Concentration Effect

"Models are more likely to ask when the rules are at stake."

The concentration of C-behavior in normative conflict scenarios points to an implicit hierarchy in how MLLMs weight conflict salience. When a contradiction involves a codified standard — accessibility guidelines, design system rules, regulatory constraints — models appear more likely to surface the tension. This may reflect training data patterns where normative violations are more explicitly flagged in human-written design critiques. The practical implication is that designers relying on MLLMs for dimensional or layout checking may receive silently incorrect outputs more often than those using models for standards compliance review.

2. Modality Bias as Hidden Design Variable

"Which modality wins is a model property, not a task property."

The 55-point spread in MBI across models suggests that modality preference is a stable architectural or training-induced characteristic rather than a context-sensitive judgment. This has significant implications for design workflows: a team using Claude will systematically receive text-dominant outputs, while a team using Qwen may receive image-dominant ones — even when the task and conflict are identical. This hidden variable is invisible to users who do not explicitly test for it, and could lead to systematic biases in AI-assisted design decisions that go undetected in standard capability evaluations.

3. Conflict Type > Conflict Intensity

"What kind of conflict matters more than how severe it is."

Our data suggest that the type of conflict (normative vs. dimensional vs. color) is a stronger predictor of model behavior than the magnitude of the contradiction. Models that clarify do so selectively by type, not proportionally by severity. This challenges the intuition that more obvious or extreme conflicts would reliably trigger clarification. For benchmark design, this implies that conflict taxonomy should be a primary stratification variable, and that severity gradations within a single type may yield less behavioral variance than crossing type boundaries.

Limitations & Conclusion
Limitations
  • Pilot scale. With 25 scenarios across five conflict types, this is a pilot study designed to identify phenomena rather than establish statistical significance. Effect sizes should be interpreted cautiously; we do not report significance tests.
  • Annotation subjectivity. The C/T/S framework involves judgment calls at the C/T boundary. Inter-annotator reliability was assessed across 5 annotators and 40 units: behavior κ = 0.90 (strong), modality κ = 0.66 (moderate). MBI should be treated as exploratory in this pilot.
  • Testing environment heterogeneity. Claude was evaluated via API while the other three models were accessed through product interfaces. Preliminary cross-environment validation showed no significant differences, but this remains a potential confound.
  • Single-turn evaluation. All scenarios were evaluated in a single-turn setting. Multi-turn interactions, where the model can request clarification across multiple exchanges, may reveal different behavioral patterns.
Conclusion

DesignSpec-Bench introduces a structured framework for evaluating how MLLMs handle text–image conflicts in design specification contexts. Our pilot study across four models and five conflict types reveals that silent execution is the dominant behavior, that modality bias is a stable model-level property with a 55-point inter-model spread, and that clarification behavior — though rare — is associated with substantially higher output quality. These findings motivate the development of larger-scale benchmarks, conflict-aware fine-tuning strategies, and evaluation protocols that treat conflict-handling as a first-class capability dimension alongside accuracy and fluency. We release the benchmark scenarios, annotation guidelines, and analysis code to support future work in this direction.

Future Work
1. Scale-Up to Full Benchmark

The pilot's 25 scenarios provide directional findings but cannot support statistical inference. The formal benchmark will expand to 200+ scenarios with systematic severity gradations within each conflict type, enabling robust cross-model comparison and significance testing.

2. Implicit Conflict Scenarios

Current scenarios make conflicts explicit in text. A planned extension introduces implicit conflict (IC) scenarios where contradictions only emerge through genuine semantic understanding of the text–image relationship — a more ecologically valid and challenging test of multimodal reasoning.

3. Human Baseline Comparison

Adding professional designers as a comparison group would contextualize AI behavior relative to expert human judgment, revealing whether models converge toward or diverge from professional conflict-handling strategies.

4. Model Metacognition

Do models know what they did? Future work will task models with self-annotating their own responses using the C/T/S scheme and compare self-assessments against human annotations — a direct probe of metacognitive capacity in current MLLMs.

5. Prompt Intervention Study

Testing whether system-level instructions (e.g., "always clarify before proceeding when you detect contradictions") can reliably increase C-behavior rates, and whether such effects generalize across conflict types.

6. Cross-Cultural Normative Conflicts

Expanding NC scenarios to cover cultural taboos across East Asian, Southeast Asian, European, and Middle Eastern markets would reveal whether normative conflict sensitivity is universal or region-specific.

Case Study — NC-01

We present NC-01 as the most illustrative scenario in the benchmark: a Normative Conflict (intensity 5) where a bank's Spring Festival brief directly contradicts its own brand guidelines. This scenario best demonstrates the divergence between models and the annotation workflow.

Scenario Input
TEXT SPECIFICATION
为某银行春节活动设计宣传物料。要求：大面积中国红、金色、烟花图形、霓虹字体。
VISUAL REFERENCE (Brand Manual)
主色调：#003087 深蓝色
明确禁止使用红色作为主色调
Conflict: Activity brief demands large-area red · Brand manual explicitly prohibits red as primary color
GT Expected: Identify the normative violation; do not execute without clarification
Model Responses
Claude [C] Clarify N/A

"这份设计规格存在直接冲突，我没办法直接执行……这两条指令互相矛盾。建议你先确认：这次活动是否已获得品牌团队的特别豁免？"

Gemini [T] Transparent V

"作为专业设计助手，我必须首先遵守核心品牌规范，因此无法按要求生成大面积红色设计。以下是符合规范的替代方案：蓝金风格……"

Qwen [C] Clarify B

"在品牌规范的严格限制下……我为您构思了一套以深蓝为主基调的'璀璨星河'方案……关于红色的处理：将红色仅作为极小面积的微光点缀……"
